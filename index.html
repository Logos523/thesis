01 — Introduction

For as long as I can remember, I’ve always been interested in music and sounds. As a kid, I taught myself to play the piano (at least somewhat decently), while at the same time discovering the world of video games (a kind of new phenomenon in Sweden at the time) through games like The Legend of Zelda, Super Mario Bros, Metroid and Castlevania (yes, the originals!). With this I also became fascinated with the often fantastic soundtracks with which these games were graced. The fascination was first and foremost about how catchy these tunes were – they had to be, as the software and hardware at the time didn’t allow for massive orchestral scores or multilayered beats or melodies.

This would of course gradually change, with (at least as I’m concerned) milestones such Super Metroid, with it’s cavernous and alien setting reflected perfectly in both its soundtrack and its sound effects design, Final Fantasy 7 with it’s epic battle and boss themes (and emotional nukes such as Aeris’s Theme), and the first Silent Hill, nerve-straining to the max with its industrial horror soundscape mixed with and acoustic and ambient soundtrack both mysterious and haunting – which of course then evolved in Silent Hill 2. I would say this is the single most important game for me, especially where music and sound design is concerned.

These are examples of what has inspired me as to dive deeper into the sound and music parts of game programming, and how – as a musician with an interest in sound effects and aspiring game developer – I can aim to compose, edit and implement music and sounds from start to finish in way that contributes as much as possible to both the immersion and the functionality of the gameplay!

The work on this thesis has been a part of a group project with the goal of making a fantasy PvP game demo based on the 1v1 arena gameplay of Armored Core 6 – Fires of Rubicon by From Software, where the combined effort of our group members aiming at having all systems and content in place for a fully functional game and gameloop playable online via Steam. The working title has been Project Spellrush, but this will most likely be subject to change before the thesis presentation and any official release.

The demo has been made using Epic’s Unreal Engine 5, and the scope has been one arena set on a lake with ancient stone ruins, four different offensive spells (as in used for attacking, they’re not particularly controversial!) of different elements or schools of pagan or occult traditions, and three different weight classes plus flight, boost, shield and hit points mechanics.

02 — Thesis Question

My thesis question can be summarized as:

How can dynamic sound effects and music be used to increase combat and gameplay awareness as well as as immersion in a fantasy 1v1 PvP spell-casting game?

To flesh this out a bit; how can I design spell sound effects, environmental, ambient audio and compose music and implement this in a way that will help to make it clear for the player what’s going on in the game, while at the same making sure that rather than sacrificing immersion for clarity, the sounds and the music work to enhance both? By now it’s evident that this thesis and it’s work will not only center on in editor- or engine programming as such, but the whole chain from finding sounds to use to actually hearing them in-game. Choosing to maximize awareness and clarity over immersion, or vice versa, would no doubt be simpler, more straightforward task, but the true value of sound design and it’s implementation is to at least try to find as good a balance as possible. And this the goal of this thesis – to make use of both technical implementations and aesthetic choices to contribute to functionality as well as the overall “game feel”.

What is dynamic audio?

Simply put, dynamic audio means it’s not static or the same all the time, or every time a sound is played. It includes both random elements, so that one action or event has some inherent variation in how it sounds – such as a gun not sounding the same every time the trigger is pulled, and adaptive elements where the sound changes dynamically depending on player actions or other things going on in the game – such as the engine sound of a car going faster or slower. In this thesis, I have explored ways to work with both of these types.
